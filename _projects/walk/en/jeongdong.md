After [Seochon](./seochon) and [Bukchon](./bukchon), I wondered where I should go next. I decided on Jeong-dong, which lies between the two neighborhoods if you follow Sejong-daero all the way south.

The name Jeong-dong comes from Jeongneung, the tomb of Queen Sindeok, the second wife of King Taejo Yi Seong-gye. Administratively, the area belongs to Sogong-dong.

Some of the places I visited today, such as City Hall Station and the Seoul Museum of Art, are technically outside Jeong-dong. For convenience, however, I have grouped the surrounding area together as Jeong-dong.

I divided the area into two sections along Sejong-daero: Deoksugung Palace on the left and Seoul City Hall's main building on the right.

Jeong-dong looks smaller than Seochon and Bukchon because many of its attractions are concentrated in a relatively small area. But once you follow the walking route, you end up covering more ground than you might expect. According to my step-counting app, I walked about 4,000 more steps than I did in Bukchon.

Today, I'll be visiting three observatories. Another point of this walk is seeing how different parts of Seoul look from each one.

## City Hall Station

Route: **[City Hall Station Exit 12]** → Deoksugung Palace (Seokjojeon, Dondeokjeon, National Museum of Modern and Contemporary Art) → Seoul City Hall Seosomun Building → Seoul Museum of Art → Jeongdong-gil → Pai Chai University History Museum → Deoksugung Palace's Jungmyeongjeon → Gojong's Road → Former Russian Legation → Cecilmaru Observatory at Jeongdong Theater

City Hall Station is a transfer station for Lines 1 and 2. It is called City Hall Station because it is located in front of Seoul City Hall.

If I leave from Sillim Station, it takes about 30 minutes to get to City Hall Station without transferring.

Most of Line 2 doesn't have themed stations like [Anguk Station](bukchon#Anguk Station) or [Gyeongbokgung Station](./seochon#Gyeongbokgung Station) on Line 3. City Hall Station on Line 2 is one of the few exceptions. Its red brick walls and columns give the station a somewhat cozy atmosphere.

![alt text](../images/시청역2호선.jpeg)

I left home rather late and didn't have much time left before my reservation at Deoksugung Palace's Seokjojeon. Without even having time to look around the area, I headed straight for Exit 12 and made my way to the palace.

Exits 2 and 3 are actually closer to Deoksugung Palace, but they are on the Line 1 side of the station. So, coming out through Exit 12 and walking from there is probably faster. (I think.)

## Around Deoksugung Palace

I didn't get a photo of Daehanmun, the main gate of Deoksugung Palace. After getting my ticket, I walked quickly toward Seokjojeon.

Admission to Deoksugung Palace is 1,000 won, and the palace is closed every Monday.

Route: City Hall Station Exit 12 → **[Deoksugung Palace's Seokjojeon]**, Dondeokjeon, National Museum of Modern and Contemporary Art → Seoul City Hall Seosomun Building → Seoul Museum of Art → Pai Chai University History Museum → Deoksugung Palace's Jungmyeongjeon → Jeongdong-gil → Former Russian Legation → Gojong's Road → Cecilmaru Observatory at Jeongdong Theater

To visit Deoksugung Palace's Seokjojeon, you need to make a guided tour reservation through the <a href="[https://royal.khs.go.kr/ROYAL/contents/R601000000.do?schGroupCode=dsg&schGroupCodeNm=덕수궁](https://royal.khs.go.kr/ROYAL/contents/R601000000.do?schGroupCode=dsg\&schGroupCodeNm=덕수궁)" target="\_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Royal Palaces and Tombs Center website\</a>.

Both general and in-depth tours are available. Today, I chose the 45-minute general tour.

Fortunately, I arrived just in time for my reservation. Before going inside, I took some photos because the palace and the crape myrtle in full bloom looked beautiful together.

![alt text](../images/덕수궁석조전과_배롱나무.jpeg)

I walked around the interior of Seokjojeon with the other visitors who had reserved the tour, listening to the guide's explanations.

Deoksugung Palace's Seokjojeon was completed in December 1910. The Korea-Japan Annexation Treaty had already been signed before its completion, meaning that the building was completed after Korea had entered the period of Japanese colonial rule.

Over the years, the building was used as the Yi Royal Family Art Museum, a venue for the U.S.-Soviet Joint Commission, and the National Museum of Modern and Contemporary Art. It is now operated as the Daehan Empire History Museum.

As the first Western-style building in the Korean Empire, it was built in the Neoclassical palace style that was popular in Europe at the time. Just as the front of the building is symmetrical, the interior is also arranged with strict symmetry.

![alt text](../images/덕수궁석조전_접견실.jpeg)

Walking around inside, you can really feel how strong that symmetry is.

The first floor was used as a workspace, while the second floor served as living quarters.;

The furniture and plants in the Empress's sitting room on the second floor are quite elaborate. I actually liked this room more than the Emperor's sitting room next door.

![alt text](../images/덕수궁석조전_황후거실.jpeg)

The grand dining room on the first floor is even more luxurious and ornate. I suppose that makes sense, since this was where banquets were held for guests.

Here, the guide used a piece of frosted glass to show us the materials used inside Seokjojeon and explained that although the exterior is made of granite, the interior is made of brick.

![alt text](../images/덕수궁석조전_대식당.jpeg)

This is the garden as seen from the second-floor terrace. It went through several changes before taking on its current appearance.

The palace columns partially blocked the view, so the photo didn't turn out quite as neatly as I had hoped.

![alt text](../images/덕수궁석조전_2층테라스.jpeg)

After finishing the guided tour, I headed to Dondeokjeon behind Seokjojeon. It had been demolished in the past and was restored by the Cultural Heritage Administration before reopening in 2023.

It can be visited free of charge without a reservation. Dondeokjeon is prettier at night than during the day.

A special exhibition titled *Banhwa, Auspicious Heart* is currently being held to commemorate the 140th anniversary of diplomatic relations between Korea and France. It runs only until August 30 this year.

![alt text](../images/덕수궁돈덕전.jpeg)

![alt text](../images/덕수궁돈덕전2.jpeg)

When you enter, you can choose one of three types of postcards.

After thinking about it for a moment, I chose the one with the most appealing flower-petal design.

![alt text](../images/덕수궁돈덕전_반화.jpeg)

Banhwa refers to decorative objects made by adorning flowers or trees with jewels and metal ornaments.

The exhibition was held to commemorate the fact that King Gojong presented a banhwa to the French president as a gift celebrating the establishment of diplomatic relations.

![alt text](../images/덕수궁돈덕전_반화2.jpeg)

After taking a quick look around Dondeokjeon, I headed to the National Museum of Modern and Contemporary Art, Deoksugung, located right in front of it.

Route: City Hall Station Exit 12 → Deoksugung Palace's Seokjojeon, Dondeokjeon, **[National Museum of Modern and Contemporary Art]** → Seoul City Hall Seosomun Building → Seoul Museum of Art → Pai Chai University History Museum → Deoksugung Palace's Jungmyeongjeon → Jeongdong-gil → Former Russian Legation → Gojong's Road → Cecilmaru Observatory at Jeongdong Theater

The exhibition *Lee Dae-won: Nothing Makes You Sad* is being held here until November 8 this year.

Before going inside, I took a photo of the crape myrtle in front of the museum. I liked the combination of the overcast sky and the deep pink flowers in full bloom.

![alt text](../images/국립현대미술관덕수궁관_배롱나무.jpeg)

Admission to the National Museum of Modern and Contemporary Art, Deoksugung, is 2,000 won. I took my time looking through Lee Dae-won's paintings.

The exhibition is divided into four sections according to different periods. The painting below depicts part of a wall and some ivy at the artist's home in 1960.

I took a photo because I liked the texture, colors, and flat composition of the painting. This calm color palette would later transform into something much more vibrant.

![alt text](../images/이대원_담쟁이.jpeg)

The title of this exhibition, *Nothing Makes You Sad*, is also the title of the final section.

In his later years, Lee Dae-won painted mountains, apple trees, and fields in his hometown of Paju. He developed a broader and deeper approach to painting through a style that was distinctly his own.

One of the descriptions on the wall near the entrance said that viewers should feel as if they are not simply looking at a painting, but stepping inside it.

So I tried looking at the paintings while imagining myself actually walking around inside them.

In the painting below, I could see a field and tree branches swaying in the wind. As I walked around the exhibition and thought about that scene, it almost felt as though I could hear the sound of the wind.

![alt text](../images/이대원_산.jpeg)

Even though it was Friday morning, the exhibition was crowded. There were far more people than usual, and visitors kept walking in front of the paintings.

So I changed my approach. Instead of trying to look at the paintings alone, I decided to watch the people and the paintings together.

There was something interesting about watching people trying to take photos or standing there, absorbed in the paintings.

After leaving the Seosomun Building, I walked along Deoksugung-gil, which is also famous for its stone-wall road.

Following the road, I reached an intersection. Just inside it was the Seoul Museum of Art.

The museum is currently hosting *Yoo Youngkuk: The Mountain Is Within Me*, *The Base of Technology: Scenes at the Boundary*, and *The Origin of Love*.

![alt text](../images/서울시립미술관_현수막.jpeg)

Yoo Youngkuk was a schoolmate of Lee Dae-won, whose exhibition I had just seen at the National Museum of Modern and Contemporary Art, Deoksugung. Both of them studied under Sato Kunio.

There was a photograph hanging in one corner of the exhibition hall. Yoo Youngkuk is on the left, and Kim Whanki is on the right, walking down a street.

I found myself wishing that the clothes people wore in those days would become fashionable again someday.

![alt text](../images/서울시립미술관_유영국김환기.jpeg)

Like the Lee Dae-won exhibition, the exhibition was organized into sections according to different periods. You could see how Yoo Youngkuk's approach to painting changed over time.

His early geometric forms gradually became more restrained compositions of triangles, straight lines, and diamonds, while the rough textures gave way to smoother ones.

After looking through the exhibitions on each floor, I left the museum. Taking the road on the left leads to the Pai Chai University History Museum.

Here is Pai Chai University as seen from the front. Looking at it now, I think I took the photo too close to capture the whole building.

![alt text](../images/배재학당.jpeg)

Pai Chai Hakdang was Korea's first modern educational institution, founded by the American missionary Henry Appenzeller during the reign of King Gojong.

I learned from the exhibition here that the word "Baehje" means "a place where useful people are raised and educated."

Historical figures such as Ju Si-gyeong, Syngman Rhee, Korea's first president, and poet Kim So-wol all graduated from Pai Chai Hakdang.

Leaving the museum and turning left brings me back to the intersection where I entered the Seoul Museum of Art earlier. I then took the road next to the Jeongdong Theater.

Route: City Hall Station Exit 12 → Deoksugung Palace's Seokjojeon, Dondeokjeon, National Museum of Modern and Contemporary Art → Seoul City Hall Seosomun Building → Seoul Museum of Art → Pai Chai University History Museum → **[Deoksugung Palace's Jungmyeongjeon]** → Jeongdong-gil → Former Russian Legation → Gojong's Road → Cecilmaru Observatory at Jeongdong Theater

![alt text](../images/덕수궁_중명전.jpeg)

Following the road beside the Jeongdong Theater brings you to Jungmyeongjeon.

Jungmyeongjeon was where Ito Hirobumi and the Five Eulsa Traitors signed the Eulsa Treaty in 1905. It was also where Gojong arranged for the Hague Secret Emissaries to be sent.

In 1904, a major fire broke out at Deoksugung Palace, then known as Gyeongungung. King Gojong moved into a library called Suokheon, where he lived and conducted state affairs. The building was later renamed Jungmyeongjeon.

Gojong opposed the treaty until the very end, but it was forced upon him. The interior exhibition explains what happened during the signing of the treaty.

At the time, Japan took away the Korean Empire's diplomatic rights under the pretext of "protecting" it and established the Japanese Residency-General to control its administration. Ito Hirobumi became its first Resident-General.

![alt text](../images/덕수궁_중명전2.jpeg)

After leaving Jungmyeongjeon, I walked along Jeongdong-gil.

Fresh green leaves hung from the branches. There wasn't a single fallen leaf along the road.

The street is lined with restaurants and cafés with a nice atmosphere. It's still humid and hot, so I imagine people can only comfortably eat outdoors after the sun goes down.

![alt text](../images/정동길.jpeg)

Route: City Hall Station Exit 12 → Deoksugung Palace's Seokjojeon, Dondeokjeon, National Museum of Modern and Contemporary Art → Seoul City Hall Seosomun Building → Seoul Museum of Art → Pai Chai University History Museum → Deoksugung Palace's Jungmyeongjeon → Jeongdong-gil → **[Former Russian Legation]** → Gojong's Road → Cecilmaru Observatory at Jeongdong Theater

Walking along Jeongdong-gil, I came across Yewon School. There is a road beside the school.

That road leads to Jeongdong Historical Park. The Former Russian Legation stands above the park.

This is where King Gojong moved after leaving Gyeongbokgung Palace in 1896 because he felt threatened following the assassination of Empress Myeongseong. This event is known as the Agwan Pacheon, or Gojong's refuge at the Russian Legation.

A year later, Gojong returned to Deoksugung Palace and established the Korean Empire, declaring its independence in an effort to free the country from foreign interference.

Walking around a place where such important moments in history took place makes me feel strangely dignified.

![alt text](../images/구러시아공사관.jpeg)

Route: City Hall Station Exit 12 → Deoksugung Palace's Seokjojeon, Dondeokjeon, National Museum of Modern and Contemporary Art → Seoul City Hall Seosomun Building → Seoul Museum of Art → Pai Chai University History Museum → Deoksugung Palace's Jungmyeongjeon → Jeongdong-gil → Former Russian Legation → **[Gojong's Road]** → Cecilmaru Observatory at Jeongdong Theater

Walking up the road beside the Former Russian Legation, I came across a gate. Crossing through it marks the beginning of Gojong's Road.

It is called Gojong's Road because it is believed to have been used by King Gojong to travel between the Russian Legation, where he stayed after the Agwan Pacheon, and Deoksugung Palace.

![alt text](../images/정동역사공원_고종의길.jpeg)

After walking along Gojong's Road, I reached the Seonwonjeon area of Deoksugung Palace.

Restoration work is currently underway.

![alt text](../images/덕수궁_선원전영역.jpeg)

There is a three-way intersection in front of the Seonwonjeon area.

Going straight leads to another section of Deoksugung's stone-wall road. It has a different charm from the stone-wall road leading toward the Seoul Museum of Art.

For years, access to this road was restricted because it ran alongside the grounds of the British Embassy. It has now been about ten years since it was opened to the public.

The British Embassy is on the left, and Deoksugung Palace is on the right.

![alt text](../images/영국대사관_덕수궁돌담길.jpeg)

Route: City Hall Station Exit 12 → Deoksugung Palace's Seokjojeon, Dondeokjeon, National Museum of Modern and Contemporary Art → Seoul City Hall Seosomun Building → Seoul Museum of Art → Pai Chai University History Museum → Deoksugung Palace's Jungmyeongjeon → Jeongdong-gil → Former Russian Legation → Gojong's Road → **[Cecilmaru Observatory at Jeongdong Theater]**

Strangely enough, this road ends at the entrance to Deoksugung Palace. Because the British Embassy occupies the area, the road leading outside is blocked, so you have to take the back path through the palace grounds.

There is a temporary passage next to the path where I encountered the cat earlier.

I came across a tree with a strangely hollow center. It looked like it had gone on an extreme diet. Maybe it had taken Mounjaro or something.

![alt text](../images/덕수궁_속이비어있는나무.jpeg)

After leaving Deoksugung Palace again, I reached the road beside Jeongdong Theater's Cecil building, where Seoul City Hall can be seen in the distance.

A little farther down, there is a random-looking elevator on the left. Taking it up leads to the rooftop observatory of Cecil Theater.

There is no admission fee, so you can visit without hesitation.

![alt text](../images/세실극장_옥상전망대.jpeg)

From the rooftop observatory, I took a photo of Seoul Anglican Cathedral.

I didn't take a photo of it, but from here you can also see people at Seoul City Hall's observatory and visitors walking around Deoksugung Palace.

Since this observatory is lower than the others, I found the view of the cathedral right next to it more interesting than the broader view of Seoul.

![alt text](../images/세실극장_옥상전망대_서울주교좌성당.jpeg)

I had originally planned to visit the Seoul Urbanism & Architecture Exhibition Hall as well, but it seemed to be undergoing interior construction, so I turned around.

Now I'll cross the street and head to Seoul City Hall.


## Around Seoul City Hall

Route: **[Seoul Plaza]** → Seoul City Hall Sky Observatory, Sky Square Gallery, My Seoul → Wongudan

This is Seoul City Hall and Seoul Library as seen from Seoul Plaza. I'll visit Seoul Library on my next walk.

Seoul Plaza was once the site of the road along the walls of Deoksugung Palace in 1910. A section of that road has been recreated here in the plaza.

I entered Seoul City Hall. Although it is a workplace for city employees, there are also many spaces that are open to the public.

The 8th and 9th floors house the Sky Observatory and Sky Square Gallery. Just like at the Seosomun Building, I took a dedicated elevator to get there.

As soon as I arrived, a live broadcast of Dokdo was playing on a TV.

The main City Hall building's Sky Observatory offers a better view of the Jeongdong Theater and Seoul Anglican Cathedral, but the building's exterior design blocks the view in places. So if you want to take in the whole area, I think the observatory at the Seosomun Building is better.

In particular, it's difficult to get a good view of Seoul Plaza from the Sky Observatory.



For reference, Seoul Library also has an outdoor observatory.

I'll have to visit it during my next walk around Jeong-dong and see what the view is like.



Route: Seoul Plaza → Seoul City Hall Sky Observatory, **[Sky Square Gallery]**, My Seoul → Wongudan

Taking the stairs down to the 8th floor, I found an exhibition called Traces of Seoul.

It was an exhibition showing the small everyday scenes and landscapes of alleys that are gradually disappearing. 

Seoul is a fascinating city. From the Joseon Dynasty and the modern era, through the Korean Empire and the Korean War, to the rapidly developing city of today, traces of its many different faces can still be found on its streets.

So far, I've walked through Seochon, Bukchon, and now Jeong-dong, seeing only small parts of what Seoul has to offer. There is still so much more to see.

There's no need to be endlessly sad about things disappearing. Places are always changing. What we see now is simply one moment preserved within that change. 

And perhaps that is precisely why we can appreciate it. Just as thinking about parting can make us realize how much we love someone.



Even after saying all that, I'm someone who has a hard time letting go, so I tend to feel a little sad about things that are passing by.

That's why I started this project. I wanted to preserve the things I've seen through writing, photographs, and a few videos, so that I could look back on them someday.

Route: Seoul Plaza → Seoul City Hall Sky Observatory, Sky Square Gallery, My Seoul → **[Wongudan]**

Wongudan is next to Seoul Center Building, the same building I saw beside Namsan Seoul Tower earlier from Seoul Plaza.

Wongudan is a site where the emperor performed rituals to Heaven. Most of it was demolished by the Japanese Government-General of Korea, leaving only parts such as Hwangungwu and the three gates.



The stairs leading up to Hwangungwu were covered in various unexplained marks, almost like bruises. I didn't particularly feel like taking a photo of them.

Above a small side gate near Hwangungwu, there is a painting of a dragon.



## In the End

Today, I walked around Deoksugung Palace and Seoul City Hall.

It is an area filled with the history of the Korean Empire, with museums, old buildings, and quiet streets all gathered together.

As you walk through the city, you begin to realize that even places you've passed by without a second thought can hold more stories than you expected.

You might discover a new path. And once you do, that place begins to feel different from before. That's the charm of taking a walk.

Everything I saw today will eventually disappear or change into something else. So perhaps simply being able to see it as it is now is valuable enough.

I'm looking forward to seeing what I'll discover next time.
