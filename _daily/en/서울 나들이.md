I visited quite a few different places this month.

A friend had an interview near Seoul Station and stayed at my place for the night, so after the interview, we went to Anguk Station and grabbed some bread.

London Bagel Museum, which was incredibly popular for a while.

Considering how expensive it is, there are plenty of fun little details to make up for it.

My favorite thing was that you can add as much sugar to your coffee as you want.

I’m not particularly into sweet foods, but I do like sugar itself quite a bit.

When I was little, I even used to climb onto the kitchen sink like a monkey and dip my finger into the sugar jar in the cupboard.

I’m a little sad I didn’t get a picture of that Americano loaded with sugar.

I also went to the Seoul Museum of Art in Seosomun for the first time in a while.

They were holding an exhibition by Yoo Youngkuk called *The Mountain Is Within Me*.

![Yoo Youngkuk exhibition](../images/유영국전시.png)

The exhibition featured abstract paintings of Korean mountains, carefully arranged in spaces that made use of light, shadow, and the surrounding environment.

“Abstraction” is the process of representing only the common characteristics of something. In that sense, it’s similar to generalization in that it reduces complexity.

In programming, abstraction refers to the process of identifying the commonalities and essential properties of objects in the real world.

Take a car, for example. A car is a means of transportation.

The ability to move forward, move backward, turn left and right, and start and stop are characteristics that can generally be applied to most means of transportation, such as trains, subways, bicycles, and boats.

So we can group these common characteristics together and abstract them into the concept of a “means of transportation.”

Abstraction in art works in a similar way. An artist can express the common characteristics of mountains, or the iconic features that define them, through painting.

The difference is that artistic abstraction can be subjective rather than realistic.

Because artists incorporate both representations of the natural world and abstract expressions of their own personal impressions into their work, abstract art can inevitably feel profound or difficult to understand at first glance.

That’s what makes abstraction interesting, but it can also be difficult to understand, and after looking at a number of works, I sometimes find myself getting a little tired.

I happened to discover a walking course called *The Road of the Korean Empire*, so I decided to walk it on another day.

I got off at City Hall Station and made my way through Jeongdong-gil, the area around Deoksugung Palace, Seoul Anglican Cathedral, King Gojong’s Road, and finally Gwanghwamun.

![The Road of the Korean Empire](../images/대한제국의_길.jpeg)

It had been a while since I’d seen Seokjojeon Hall and the gardens at Deoksugung.

The National Museum of Modern and Contemporary Art, Deoksugung, right next door, was under construction, so I couldn’t visit.

![Seokjojeon Hall and the garden at Deoksugung](../images/덕수궁_석조전.jpeg)

After leaving Deoksugung, if you head over to the nearby National Jeongdong Theater Cecil, there’s an elevator inside.

Take it up to the rooftop and you’ll find the Cecil Rooftop Observatory.

Seoul Anglican Cathedral is right in front of the observatory, so you get a surprisingly foreign-looking view in the middle of Seoul.

![Cecil Rooftop Observatory](../images/세실옥상전망대.jpeg)

I wandered around here and there along the course and eventually made my way out to Gwanghwamun.

A café I had visited once before.

I’d tried to go back afterward but couldn’t remember where it was, so I never found it again. Finally, I found it.

I hope there comes a day when I can go back again.

![Gwanghwamun café](../images/광화문_카페.jpeg)

It was also nice to see Gwanghwamun Gukbap again after so long.
